The Coco · rung 6

Stake to Settlement

A token staked in one block, traced to a settled verdict — every proof-of-stake and BFT function it passes through, which process runs which goal, and what row each step leaves behind. Who may vote is a query over the chain, a quorum is a counting rule, and a block a quorum precommitted is final — which is where a longer chain stops being an argument.

The split that shapes all of it: the federation roster answers whose key is this. The chain answers how much do they weigh. Two questions, two sources — and dave is why they are two: he is in the federation, his signature verifies perfectly, and his vote counts for nothing.

01

Staking, and reading it back

ledger_seal/1 → stake_from_chain/0 → learn_stake/1

A validator's weight is a payload. stake(alice,40) is sealed as an ordinary block, so the entry is hash-committed, signed by an authority, gossiped like anything else and identical on every node — and none of that needed a new mechanism, because rung 2 already does all of it.

  1. 1Seal it. Four blocks, four entries. The founding authority signs them, because a chain with no stake on it has no quorum to admit any. ledger_seal('stake(alice,40)') → block_hash/5 → secp256k1_sign/3
  2. 2Gossip it. Every node re-verifies every block it is offered, exactly as it would any other. A stake entry is not a special kind of block and is not treated as one. ledger_export/0 → ledger_sync/1 → valid_block/6
  3. 3Read it back. Walk the blocks, parse the payloads that are stake entries, skip the ones that are not — the chain carries contracts and submissions too, and a rule that choked on somebody else's payload would only work on a chain used for one thing. stake_from_chain/0 · stake_payload/3 → learn_stake/1
  4. 4Count each block once. Entries accumulate on purpose — a top-up is a second block, not an edit — so nothing can tell a genuine second entry from the same entry read twice except the block it came from. stake_seen(Hash) — the block, not the entry

In the choreography, alice seals every entry and bob reads them. bob was never handed a roster of weights and never asked anyone what the validator set is. He read blocks he already held. That is the difference between rung 2 and rung 6 in one sentence: an authority is declared in a file every node is given, and a validator is a query over rows every node already has.

02

The draw, and who is allowed to check it

leader/3 · draw_index/3 · proposal_ok/2

A validator with twice the stake occupies twice the interval and is drawn twice as often. The suite checks the ordering rather than the counts: alice drawn most, mallory least. Pinning exact counts would be testing sha256, not the draw.

And the voter checks the proposer from the block's own parent

proposal_ok/2 re-derives the draw using the block's own parent hash as the seed — not the reader's current head. So “was this author drawn for this height” has the same answer forever, on every node, including one reading the chain a year later. A rule that depended on the reader's head would give different answers to different readers, which is not a rule.

03

Two phases, and the lock

do_prevote/3 → learn_pol/3 → do_precommit/3

Without the second phase, two quorums at one height need nobody to have done anything provably wrong. With it, they need a third of the stake to have ignored its own rule — and that is the difference between a protocol that is safe and one that is merely usually right.

A POL is not taken on anyone's word. The prevote certificate is checked by this validator against this validator's own view of the stake, and only then does it become a reason to change a lock. A lock that could be released by an unverified claim is not a lock.

There is no round timer, no leader announcement, no aggregator role and no daemon. A validator is a cocolog invocation that reads rows, casts one vote and exits. What another validator has voted is a row it can read, which is why a certificate can be assembled by anyone at all — and why two nodes holding the same votes build the same one.

04

The counting rule, and why two thirds

qc_valid/1 · quorum/2 · culprits/3

A quorum certificate is a list of signatures and a predicate that says whether the list is enough. There is no message type and no protocol. Every clause of qc_valid/1 is a way the list can be a lie:

the checkthe lie it answers
every vote matches the certificate's kind, height, round and blocka pile of votes for four different blocks, offered as a quorum for one
every vote is valid — staked voter, real signaturea stranger's vote, or a signature that was never made
as many distinct voters as votesone validator's vote repeated — the cheapest attack here, and the one a length check would pass
the voters' stake reaches the quoruma majority of names holding a minority of the stake

Byzantine fault tolerance here is not “the bad case cannot happen”. It is “the bad case names the validators who caused it” — and a name is what a slashing rule needs.

equivocation/3 is the sharper form of the same idea. A validator who signs two different blocks at one height and round has produced, with its own key, a pair of documents nobody else could have made. Both votes are individually valid and no checker looking at one could say a thing; what she cannot do is stop the pair existing. There is nothing to corroborate and nobody to believe.

05

Finality beats length

extends_final/1 — rung 6's one added rule

Rung 2's fork choice may revisit any tip: a longer chain wins, and “longer” is a fact that can change tomorrow. extends_final/1 is one rule — a chain that omits a finalised block is not a candidate at all — and fork choice may then prefer whatever it likes among the chains that pass.

The first version of this demonstration proved nothing, and the fix was to the arrangement rather than the rule. mallory had already gossiped alice's block, so she extended the finalised chain instead of forking from its parent — and the page would have printed “yes, the longer tip contains it”. A partition is a gossip list that is short for a while, so who a node hears from became an argument. A demonstration that cannot fail is not a demonstration.

06

mallory, from inside the validator set

votes/mallory.pl — seven refused, one accepted

Every earlier rung's criminal was an outsider: she sealed blocks without being an authority, wrote contracts that were fenced out, submitted models she had not trained. Here she is admitted, staked and entitled to vote on every block — because that is what a Byzantine fault is, and a rung about tolerating faults that only tested strangers would have tested nothing.

She cannot make two certificates alone, and the attack does not pretend she can. Fifteen is nowhere near sixty-seven, and even her best pairing reaches fifty-five — so she buys alice and carol. Both certificates are real: a node holding either one is not being deceived about anything. What the arithmetic guarantees is that they could not both exist without an overlap heavier than a third of the stake.

The grind succeeds and is supposed to. The leader is a function of the head's hash, and the head's hash is a function of the payload of the block that made it — so a proposer tries payloads until the next draw favours her. With fifteen per cent of the stake she expects about seven attempts; here the first payload that worked was the twenty-fourth. There is nothing to detect: every payload she tried was legitimate, and the one she published is a legitimate block.

That is the price of a schedule anyone can recompute from rows. Inside a certificate-gated federation — named parties who had to be admitted and can be removed — biasing your own turn is a cost worth paying for a draw with no beacon, no committee and no extra round of messages. Outside one it is not, and it would want a VRF or an unbiasable beacon. The trade is stated where it is made, in library(pos).

07

Till settlement complete

where rung 6 hands back to rungs 2–4

Nothing downstream changed. The payload under all of this is still a contract's source or a training submission whose weights travel as chunked rows and whose digest is the join, and settlement still asks the only question with a checkable answer — does it work.

What rung 6 adds is that the chain settlement reads is no longer merely the deepest one anybody happened to see. “This submission was on the chain” stops being a fact that can be revised — and a verdict computed over a finalised prefix is a verdict that does not have to be recomputed if a longer fork turns up next week.

What this rung does not add

not thiswhy
a slashing ruleculprits/3 and equivocation/3 produce the evidence. Burning a bond — how much, on whose judgement, with what appeal — is a policy question this rung takes no position on.
a liveness argumentthere is no round timer, so rounds advance because the choreography advances them. Deciding when a validator gives up on a proposer needs a clock, and the honest form of a clock here is rung 5's spine, not wall time.
a genesis distributionthe first stake entries are sealed by a founding authority, because a chain with no stake on it has no quorum to admit any. Bootstrapping is a real problem; this rung inherits rung 2's federation for exactly one block's worth of trust.
an unbiasable drawsee attack_grind. Stated where the trade is made rather than left for a reader to discover.
